01 · the problem

Finding a crowd to watch the game with is guesswork.

Transplants, fans of out-of-town teams, and anyone whose friends don't follow their sport have no easy way to know where fellow fans gather. Bars advertise TVs, not who'll be there.

The solution: pick your team, then see how many fans are checked in at each spot nearby, on a list and a map.

02 · my approach

Build first, then test like a designer.

I skipped formal research and went straight into building with Claude Code.

To keep that honest, I logged every assumption as a question to test, and reviewed the working prototype with standard UX methods.

  1. AssumeLog each assumption and the decision it drives.
  2. BuildA working prototype, one feature at a time.
  3. EvaluateA heuristic review of every screen.
  4. ListenInformal feedback from someone using it.
  5. CompareCompeting concepts, built for real.
  6. RefineSmall changes, each with a reason.

04 · design principles

Five principles settled most arguments.

Value before sign-up

Browse freely. Sign up only to check in or review.

Honest numbers

One check-in per person per game, gone when it ends.

Honest about the demo

Every page says what's made up and what's real.

Accessible by default

WCAG 2.1 AA from the first plan, not a later pass.

A brand that stays out of the way

One orange. Team colors appear only on team badges.

06 · iteration

Four navigation concepts, built for real.

Why: a tester found the single long page tiring. Teams were at the top and spots far below, so changing your mind meant scrolling up and down.

Every concept had to: show which step you're on, make going back easy, and require a team before showing spots.

concept a

Step by step

Three pages, with Back and Next fixed to the bottom.

kept: the progress line and check marks
dropped: one page too many

concept b

Two tabs

Team and Bars tabs pinned to the top. Bars stays locked until you pick a team.

kept: the sticky bar and its updating subtext
dropped: the next step was easy to miss

concept c

Tap and go

Tapping a team jumps straight to its spots.

dropped: moving on instantly surprises people

concept d · shipped

Best of both

A's progress line inside B's sticky bar, plus a button that fades in at the bottom once a team is picked.

became the live site after four rounds of refinement

Before and after

Earlier builds beside the live site, set to the same moment of the same game.

Picking a team

From the first build to the Team step.

  • The sport dropdown became one row of filter buttons.
  • Team chips became cards, with a star to save a team.
  • A step bar and a button that appears once a team is picked show what comes next.

Finding a spot

Picking a team on the old single page scrolled you down to the game, and your teams left the screen.

  • The step bar keeps Team and Games one tap away.
  • The game card is half the height, so the map shows without scrolling.
  • Pins replaced dots on the map.

On a phone

The same moment on a 390px-wide screen.

  • The header and step bar stay on screen.
  • The map starts higher up, on the first screen.

A spot's page

Rebuilt after trying several layouts side by side.

  • The rating moved up next to the name, so it's read in order.
  • The back link says where it goes: "Where the fans are", not "Back to home".
  • Check in and Switch team sit together in the game box.
07 · accessibility

WCAG 2.1 AA from the first draft.

Contrast tested in code

4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for icons, in dark and light mode.

Never color alone

Status and errors always have an icon and words.

Keyboard first

Everything works with the keyboard, with a visible focus ring.

Screen readers

Real headings, labelled fields, and named icon buttons.

Reduce motion

Confetti and slides switch off when it's on.

Touch targets

At least 44px, on screens down to 320px wide.

What I learned

A new way to prototype. While I have used AI for other aspects of UX design, including making unbiased questions for user interviews, summarizing meetings, and synthesizing insights, I had never used it to build anything. This project exposed me to another way of prototyping where I can quickly build and test solutions myself.

Clearer prompts. I also learned more about how to write clear and effective prompts to get the results I wanted. I became more specific with what I told the agent and made sure to provide enough context for it to understand the point of the requests I ask for. I have only used chatbots for spontaneous questions before taking on this project, so this experience taught me how to interact with AI in a new way.

More concepts, better decisions. One strategy that made it easy to make more informed design decisions with Claude was telling it to generate multiple concepts. At first, I was working with only one concept, which limited idea exploration. Later, I learned that I could ask Claude to create multiple concepts, allowing me to weigh design decisions as I would in a full design project.
