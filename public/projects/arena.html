3D Arena Map

Arenas are hard to find your way around. I designed and shipped a phone-first 3D map that starts from your seat or gate and walks you to what you need.

Open the arena ↗ (opens in a new tab)
// role
UX designer; I directed Claude Code, which wrote the code
// platform
Responsive web, designed for phones and tablets first
// tools
Claude Code, TypeScript, three.js, Google Cloud Run
// the problem

In a building with several floors, it's hard to tell where you are, what's nearby and how to get there.

// what I did

Built a working 3D arena, tried each version, and redesigned how people get directions.

// outcome so far

A live prototype, with routes to every public place checked automatically. Testing with people is next.

01 · problem & context

Where am I, and how do I get there?

An arena stacks seats, food, restrooms and exits around a loop, on several floors. A sign or a flat map shows one floor at a time, and you still have to work out where you are on it.

// who it's for

A fan at a game, phone in hand, on the way to the arena or already inside. They know their seat or gate, but not the building.

// why it matters to fans

Time spent lost is time away from the game. Some also need a route with no stairs.

// where it could live

Inside a team's app or website, where fans may already go for tickets and game info.

A proto-persona: assumed, not yet checked with real fans.

The setting: a made-up arena in no particular place, with four floors and 337 places, from gates and seating sections to restrooms.

Real arenas

The assumption that shaped the design

It will almost certainly be used on a phone, on the way to the arena or inside it.

Someone who is walking needs one instruction at a time and a map that keeps up. That turned a map with a directions form into a walk you follow.

03 · ideation & decisions

From reading directions to following them.

I didn't compare separate concepts side by side here. I took one working idea through several rounds, and replaced the parts that didn't hold up.

Getting directions

The first build asked people to fill in a form. I removed it, because the app can already know both ends of the trip.

  • Explore and Directions tabs became one flow: pick a place, then get directions.
  • "Step-free route" became "Accessible route", a term everyone can understand.
  • Distance stays. The time estimate is gone, because foot traffic can change how long a walk takes.

Following the route

This is the decision that changed the project. The first guide was a stack of cards to read; now the map walks with you.

  • The walk plays continuously, and the instruction changes by itself.
  • Each step is a short heading instead of a paragraph.
  • The camera pulls back far enough to show what is around you.

Telling floors apart

Stacked floors hid each other, so I spread them out.

  • Pick a floor, and the others fade to gray outlines.
  • The dark background and no trees leave only the building.
  • A square corner and an angled one make it read as a real arena, not an oval.

First screen

Less to read before you can start.

  • Four rows of categories became one row that scrolls sideways.
  • The sheet can be dragged, and follows your finger as you scroll.
  • A "You're here" row says where you are before you ask.

04 · final design & shipped prototype

Find it, tap it, walk there.

The whole flow on a phone, from a seat in Section 118 to Section 313, two floors up:

Edge and error states

Design system

One blue for the trip

Your dot, your route and the main button share one blue.

Color by kind of place

Food, restrooms and shops each have their own color, always with an icon.

Color is focus

The floor you're on is in color. The others are gray and nearly transparent.

Accessibility

Never map-only

Destinations are also in the list or search, so the 3D view is never the only way.

No stairs, checked

Automated tests confirm accessible routes never use stairs or escalators.

Contrast

White icons on every pin color are at least 4.6:1.

Keyboard

Arrow keys change steps, Esc ends the walk, and / jumps to search.

Screen readers

New instructions are marked up to be announced. Not yet tested with a screen reader.

Motion you control

Pause, go back or skip ahead at any point in the walk.

A working build

This isn't a set of mockups. The map, the routes and the walk all run from one set of place data, so the line you follow matches the floor you see.

Where this could live

I see this as part of a team's app or website, not an app of its own. The fan's ticket could set the starting seat, the team's colors could replace the green, and the food and shop listings could come from the arena. A real version would also need the venue's floor plans and a way to locate people indoors.

What's next

  • Usability test: can people find a place and follow the walk while standing in a crowd?
  • One-finger rotating on phones. Turning the map there takes two fingers now, which not everyone can do.
  • Nearest first: sort the list by distance, now that the app has a starting point.
  • Feet or meters? Distances are in meters, with no way to switch yet.
