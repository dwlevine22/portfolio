Card Payment Redesign

Paying a credit card means choosing an amount, often without seeing what that choice costs. I'm redesigning the payment flow and adding a feature that shows the cost while you choose.

// role
UX designer; I directed Claude Code, which wrote the code
// status
In progress: first build working, amount step being redesigned
// platform
Mobile app flow, as a web prototype
// tools
Pencil and paper, Claude Code, HTML, CSS and JavaScript
// the redesign

The payment flow, reordered: amount before date, with the balance and due date where you need them.

// the new feature

What each amount costs, shown at the moment you choose it.

// outcome so far

A working first build and sketches for the next one. Not yet tested with people.

An unsolicited concept, using Capital One's app as its reference. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Capital One. The prototype is unbranded, and its card, accounts and amounts are made up.

01 · problem & context

The cost is on the statement. The choice is somewhere else.

I reviewed my own Capital One app and my January 2026 statement. The statement says how long paying only the minimum takes and what it adds up to. The two app screens where I'd pick an amount didn't show any cost.

// who it's for

Someone who can't or won't pay in full this month, choosing an amount on their phone.

// why it matters to them

The amount they choose decides how long the debt lasts and how much interest they pay.

// why it's hard for a bank

Interest is income. A design that helps customers pay less of it needs a reason the bank would want it, such as trust.

A proto-persona: assumed, not yet checked with real cardholders.

What works already

I kept a lot on purpose. The layout is simple and easy to read, contrast is strong, and Slide to pay helps prevent accidental payments.

What I'm changing

before · Capital One's order
  1. Pay to
  2. Pay from
  3. Pay on (date)
  4. Amount, grayed out until a date is picked
  5. Slide to pay
after · my order
  1. Pay to, with the balance beside it
  2. Pay from
  3. Payment amount, with what it costs
  4. Pay date, starting on today
  5. Slide to pay

My account showed no payment due, so someone with a payment due may see something different.

02 · research & synthesis

Showing the cost isn't automatically enough.

I haven't interviewed cardholders. I used published studies, public data, and a close review of one real payment flow. Claude gathered the sources.

what I foundsourcewhat it means here
About 15% of general-purpose card accounts paid only the minimum in a given cycleCFPB, 2025 report (2024 data)The minimum is a common choice, worth designing for
People shown a minimum repaid less: £99 on average, against £175 without itStewart, 2009 (mock bill)A visible minimum may pull payments down, so its cost has to be visible too
The US statement warning moved about 0.4 percentage points of accounts to the 36-month amountAgarwal and others, 2015This warning, on its own, did little
Removing the minimum from a direct debit setup changed choices, but not total repayments over 7 monthsFCA field trial, 2018Measure what people pay, not just what they tap
Apple Card already estimates interest for the amount you pickAppleThe idea isn't new; the way it's explained has to earn its place

The insight that shaped the design

The cost exists. It just isn't where the decision happens.

Moving it there is my bet, not a proven fix. Studies of cost warnings found small effects, so this needs a real test before I'd claim it helps.

03 · ideation & decisions

From a line of text to a picture of the cost.

I started on paper. The first sketch put the amount before the date and the balance next to the card, so you don't have to go back to check it.

Round 1: explain each amount in words

The first build put a cost line under every option, then a small table under the amount you chose.

Round 2: show it instead

I'm replacing those lines with a total payment calculator (sketches 2 and 3). It borrows the bar people know from checking phone storage: your payment, the balance that's left, and the interest added along the way. I'm deciding whether it becomes the amount screen itself.

Why the minimum stays

The research could be read as a reason to hide the minimum. I'm keeping it, so people know the least they need to pay right then and there. I'd rather show how paying only the minimum affects the total over time than take the option away. The choice stays with them.

04 · final design & prototype

What's settled so far.

These parts of the first build are staying. The amount step in between is being redesigned, so it isn't shown as final.

Edge and error states

Design system

Blue means you can act

One blue for links, the calendar, the slider and the chosen date.

Status has words

Green, amber and red notes always come with an icon and a sentence.

Motion with a job

Popups slide up and down, and can be dragged closed.

Accessibility

Sliding isn't required

Press Enter on Slide to pay instead. A plain tap doesn't pay.

Contrast, measured

11 of 12 sampled text styles are at least 4.5:1. The other is the grayed-out Slide to pay, at 4.39:1.

Small screens

Laid out and checked at 320, 375 and 390 pixels wide.

What's next

  • Build the calculator and settle how the bar is scaled, so a longer payoff looks longer.
  • Word the minimum carefully. It shrinks as the balance does, so "the same amount every month" doesn't describe it.
  • Add a second situation: someone already carrying a balance from an earlier statement.
  • Test it with cardholders, and compare what they'd pay, not just what they say.
